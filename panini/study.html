01"Most frequent" has three different answers

The tradition's own economy rule — anuvṛtti and adhikāra — means the terms you need most are usually not printed in the sūtra at all. Counting only written tokens gives the wrong answer. All three channels are counted as document frequency: the number of distinct sūtras in which a term is required.

The headline: प्रत्यय pratyaya is the most frequent technical term in the tradition — it governs 1,821 of 3,983 sūtras (46%) while being written in only 22 of them. Then प्रातिपदिक prātipadika 1,190, तद्धित taddhita 1,115, अङ्ग aṅga 613, धातु dhātu 610. Conversely च ca is the most written word (847 sūtras = 21%) but it is punctuation. Raw string frequency and real reading pressure are almost opposites here.

02How to count and measure

A defensible pipeline. Steps 1, 2 and 4 are where naive counts go wrong — each of these was an actual failure mode in building this study.

Pin the corpus, record the hash

DSG page = dsg_page.html, Aṣṭādhyāyī = panini_data.txt. Upstream drifts: the estate's graph was built against SHA 4d46f436…; today's upstream is 6a22591f…. Without pinning, none of the numbers reproduce.

Count terms, not word forms

Use the tradition's own padaccheda (pc) rather than a generic tokenizer; Sanskrit sandhi merges words. Never strip the Devanāgarī virāma — it is structural: removing it turns प्रत्ययः into परतयय and silently corrupts every count.

Separate the three channels

pc explicit · an inherited by anuvṛtti · ad governing adhikāra. Report each, plus the union. Implicit readings (24,131) outnumber explicit ones (13,068) by ~1.8 : 1.

Use document frequency, not tokens

# distinct sūtras requiring the term, not raw occurrences. One sūtra quoting a term three times is one learning event, not three. Compute a coverage curve over the ranked list.

Exclude function words

Your DIFFICULTY_ORDERING_RESULT.md already measured this: within content vocabulary τ = 0.887, but the decisive curation move is deleting high-frequency function words. Here the top written words are exactly those operators — teach structurally, never SRS them.

Triangulate across corpora

DSG citation density & entry length (commentarial importance) · DCS lemma frequency (genre bias: epic/kāvya-heavy) · textbook first-lesson order (τ ≈ 0.05–0.10 vs frequency, ≈ 0.45–0.84 between textbooks). Agreement between channels is the confidence signal.

Validate the headword list

DSG writes सञ्ज्ञा / सङ्ख्या, not संज्ञा / संख्या, and its digitisation has no standalone वृद्धि or अङ्ग entry. A naive headword-join drops two of the top ten terms. Always reconcile against the source text.

Report dispersion & register

Give per-1,000-sūtra rates, dispersion across adhyāyas, and the genre of the corpus. Zipf is steep here: the top 10 forms already cover 30% of required-term readings.

Cumulative coverage — how many forms do you actually need?

top 10 → 30% · top 50 → 48% · top 100 → 56% · top 300 → 67% · top 1000 → 79%

A steep head, a very long tail: 6,809 distinct forms, but ~1,000 cover four-fifths of the readings. The tail is almost entirely affix names — inventory, not vocabulary.

03Why 4,383 entries are not a syllabus

Abhyankar's own introduction says Pāṇini has "about a hundred technical terms", more than 200 suffixes, ~2,000 roots and 5,000+ special words. The Aṣṭādhyāyī dataset independently tags 88 distinct saṃjñā definitions — a striking match. So the fix is filter by type, then order by frequency, not shrink the dictionary.

The longest DSG entries — it (4,900 chars), pada (3,709), upasarga (3,277), svara (2,933), dhātu (2,591), adhikāra (2,560) — are the real conceptual core, and they confirm this batching.

119 terms in seven batches. Batches B0–B5 (102 terms) are the learn-by-heart core; B6 is a read-once reference layer. 05What never goes into a batch

Lookup on demand. DSG is the reference shelf, not a course. Keep it one click away (samskrtam.ru/sanskrit-lexicon/dsg/) for:
  • the ~858 individual affix names — ghañ, ṭhak, ṇvul, kta, lyuṭ, matup, ṭāp… — learn the system (B2), look up the label when it appears;
  • augments, substitutes, roots and gaṇa lists (213 + ~2,000 + 250+ classes in the tradition);
  • the 842 author/work names, Prātiśākhya and non-Pāṇinian-school vocabulary (Kātantra, Cāndra, Jainendra, Haima);
  • Vedic accent and chanting specifics until the student actually reads Vedic text.
Suggested pacing: B0 in one sitting (it is a code, not a list) → one batch per 2–3 weeks, each paired with reading ~30 real sūtras → begin B5–B6 only when the learner can parse a Siddhāntakaumudī derivation. For SRS, drill B1–B5 only; exclude B0 and B6.

Aṣṭādhyāyī — ashtadhyayi-com/data, sutraani/data.txt — 3,983 sūtras with padaccheda / anuvṛtti / adhikāra fields.
Study-order layers — SanskritGrammar/data/panini_sutra_graph/study_order.tsv (topological layers over the reduced uttara-pūrva graph).

Limitations. The DSG composition split is a first-definition keyword heuristic, not a manual classification. Curated term counts for "family" rows (e.g. upadhā / ṭi / anta / ādi, karman) aggregate several related word forms and are therefore upper bounds. Single-syllable operators use exact word-form matching. The two corpus snapshots have different upstream revisions from the estate's committed Pāṇini graph, so layer numbers are an ordering hint only. No learning-gain experiment was run: this is an ordering study.

Addendum. These three channels are Aṣṭādhyāyī-internal structural positions, not corpora: they do not measure frequency in grammatical treatises, and DCS was never a measured column. Both are measured separately in the companion study external_channels.html (grammar-corpus frequency, DSG salience, DCS as a register control).
